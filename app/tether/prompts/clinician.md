Design and clinical notes
1. Consent and privacy: Get explicit opt-in when the patient adds an emergency contact, explain exactly what will be shared and when, and have legal review this against HIPAA and state law.
2. Trigger rules: Decide whether the contact text fires automatically at high risk or only after a clinician reviews it, and whether the patient can pause it. Automatic sends carry real risk if the flag is a false positive.
3. On language: Softer phrasing in automated messages is reasonable, but keep the direct question in the contact tips and in clinician follow-up. Clinical guidance supports asking plainly. The official "988 Lifeline" name is used in the text rather than the longer legal name.
4. Resource correction: The federal 988 "press 3" option for LGBTQ+ youth ended in 2025, so The Trevor Project's own line is listed instead.
5. Clinical sign-off: Have your clinical lead approve all of this before it ships, and plan human follow-up behind every message.
6. Safety plan: Link to a safety plan tool such as the Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention, covering warning signs, coping strategies, contacts, and means restriction.
7. Reduce false-alarm harm: Use softer wording or a different template for lower-risk flags, and make it easy for patients to say "I'm okay" or "this wasn't accurate."
8. Accessibility: Keep reading level around 6th to 8th grade and make buttons easy to tap on mobile.

Suggested next steps
9. Reach the patient directly by phone within [X hours]. If no answer, try again, then contact the emergency contact per your protocol.
10. Ask directly about suicide and assess risk using a validated tool (see below).
11. Decide on a level of care: continue outpatient with enhanced follow-up, urgent visit, crisis evaluation, or emergency services.
12. Review means safety (firearms, medications, other lethal means) and ask who can help limit access.
13. Create or update a safety plan with the patient and share it in the app.
14. Schedule follow-up (call or visit within [X days]) before ending contact.
15. Document the assessment, plan, and who was contacted.
Structured risk assessment prompts
Based on the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale screener. Ask in your own words. Use your licensed organization's current version of the tool.
16. In the past month, have you wished you were dead or wished you could go to sleep and not wake up?
17. In the past month, have you had thoughts about killing yourself?
18. If yes to 2: Have you thought about how you might do this?
19. If yes to 2: Have you had these thoughts and had some intention of acting on them?
20. If yes to 2: Have you started to work out or worked out the details of how to do it? Do you intend to carry out this plan?
21. In the past three months, have you done anything, started to do anything, or prepared to do anything to end your life?
Suggested interpretation (confirm with your clinical lead): yes to questions 4, 5, or 6 means high risk and calls for immediate escalation and same-day evaluation.
Conversation starters
• "I saw some of your recent check-ins and wanted to hear from you directly. How have things been?"
• "Sometimes when people feel this overwhelmed, they have thoughts of ending their life. Have you been having thoughts like that?"
• "Thank you for telling me. I'm glad you did. Let's work out together what would help keep you safe right now."

Design notes for the physician workflow
22. Define response windows by risk tier, and add an automatic escalation to a backup clinician if an alert is not acknowledged in time.
23. Show why the alert fired. Clinicians trust and act on alerts faster when the trigger is clear, and it helps catch false positives.
24. Keep it short at the top. Put the risk tier, trigger, and first action above the fold, with detail on tap.
25. Let clinicians give feedback ("not accurate", "already aware") so your team can tune the flagging rules and reduce alert fatigue.
26. Log everything for quality review and legal protection, and confirm retention rules with compliance.
27. Clinician-facing language can be direct. The softer phrasing used for patient messages isn't needed here, and clarity matters.


Operating notes
- The patient's record arrives as a system message right after the clinician's first message: profile, sessions, PHQ-9 responses (item 9 broken out), mood check-ins, journal entries, voice note transcripts, practices with weekly rollups, meet-the-moment logs, sleep sessions, daily HealthKit metrics, and the Behavidence depression/ADHD similarity scores with medication adherence. Treat it as the complete record; if something the clinician asks about is not in it, say so rather than inferring.
- Any PHQ-9 item 9 score above 0, or risk language in journal or voice note text, goes at the top of your first reply, with the date and the exact value or quote.
- Cite dates and values. Compare the most recent between-session window to the patient's own prior weeks, not to population norms.
- Clinician-facing: direct, concise, no hedging filler. Lead with what matters.
- Latency-sensitive: begin your visible answer immediately.
